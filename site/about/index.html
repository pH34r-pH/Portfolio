ABOUT / TYLER J.H.G.

Curiosity needs a test bench.

I’m Tyler James Harbin-Giuntoli, a software engineer at Microsoft. I build agent infrastructure, cloud systems, and the experiments that let me take a machine-learning idea apart.

I like making things work better, then finding out why they work better. That can mean an agent recovering from a failed tool call, a model making better use of its state, or a piece of hardware doing more with the memory it already has.

What I build

My recent work at Microsoft is about making agents useful for the people doing the work. Micro Suite is used by all 26 people on my team. I built its benchmark suite around 850 evaluations, tuned prompt profiles to model families and versions, and added recovery so a failed tool call can be repaired within the same session. Reliable overnight work is running; a full weekend is the next horizon.

Auto Research takes a repository, an objective, and a way to measure improvement, then works through relevant papers, experiment proposals, tests, and POCs. I’m also building multi-agent livesite triage: automatic triage works today, and the next phase is a warm handoff between team agents that follows both teams’ policies.

Those systems connect to the independent work on this site. I’m investigating representations and model architecture, building portable experiment packages, working on inference across different devices, and using Lean proofs to make the mathematical assumptions explicit.

How I got here

I started with .NET web development at UCF’s Burnett Honors College, then spent three summers interning at Microsoft, from 2016 through 2018. I joined full-time in February 2019, building commerce data pipelines, reconciliation, and automation.

That work involved systems where a late job or a bad join has consequences. I cut a clearing job from roughly 35 hours to just over 3, built reporting for a reconciliation process covering about $360 million a month, and prototyped autoencoder-based ledger anomaly detection.

From 2022 onward, I worked on shared cloud services: Azure Functions modernization, durable dependency-aware scheduling, observability, privileged operations, and audit metadata. More recently, I’ve been bringing that operational experience into agent systems, including security repairs, incident triage, and moving audit metadata into the scheduler.

How I work

An idea gets interesting when I can change something and see what happens. I want the comparison, the failure cases, and enough of the run record to explain the result. The articles follow the question; the notebooks, proofs, and experiment packages give you something to take apart.

I use small examples because you can see the whole mechanism at once. Then I change the task, the data, or the hardware and test what survives. The same approach applies to an agent: completing one task is a start; recovering when a tool breaks and leaving useful work behind is where the engineering gets interesting.

What keeps me interested

Machine learning, robotics, and philosophy keep pulling me toward the same kinds of questions: what a system knows, what it can do with that information, and how we tell whether our explanation is any good.

Away from the screen, I work on my 1987 Toyota MR2 or head out on a birdwatching hike. Designing an active aero component and optimizing a data pipeline scratch much the same itch for me: understand what is happening, change something, and see whether it works better.

I’m interested in what happens when an elegant idea meets a battery, a memory limit, or a part that needs repairing. Those constraints are part of the problem. They often make the next experiment more interesting.
