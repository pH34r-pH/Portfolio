OPTICAL ID / TJHG

I’m Tyler James Harbin-Giuntoli. I build systems where correctness, recovery, and evidence matter—and increasingly I use those habits to ask harder questions about machine learning itself.

Becoming a parent changed the calculation for me. I had a daughter and realized that spending my career on the safest available path was no longer enough; if I was going to trade that much of my life for work, I wanted the work to matter. The research on this site is part of that pivot.

I build systems that have consequences.

My work at Microsoft has ranged from commerce and reconciliation to shared cloud infrastructure and agent systems. The scheduler I wrote now sits in a path managing tens of billions of dollars every month, and current work is pushing the end-to-end SLA toward one hour. Earlier, I cut a clearing job from roughly 35 hours to just over 3 and built reporting around a reconciliation process covering hundreds of millions of dollars a month.

More recently I’ve been working on agent infrastructure. Micro Suite is used by everyone on my team. It has accumulated thousands of evaluations; the roughly 850-evaluation figure is specifically the stabilization pass used to determine what prompt/profile adjustments different model families and versions needed. Recovery work lets failed tool calls be repaired inside the same session instead of turning every failure into a restart.

The method is adversarial.

I don’t want an idea protected because I like it. I want the strongest possible attempt to destroy it: matched controls, alternative explanations, counterexamples, formal assumptions where they help, and enough provenance to reconstruct what actually happened. Anything that survives that treatment becomes more interesting.

That discipline is the connective tissue between production engineering and the independent research here. A service that only works on the happy path is unfinished. An ML result that only survives the comparison that produced it is unfinished too.

Now I’m aiming that discipline at models.

I’m investigating representation and model architecture, building portable experiment packages, working on inference across heterogeneous hardware, and using formal proofs when a mathematical claim is cheaper to settle once than rediscover through another training run.

I prefer small mechanisms I can see completely, then harsher tests that ask what survives when the task, data, architecture, or hardware changes.

The constraints are part of the fun.

Away from the screen, I work on my 1987 Toyota MR2 or go birdwatching. Designing a mechanical component, repairing aging hardware, and debugging a distributed system all hit the same nerve: understand the mechanism, change something deliberately, and see whether reality agrees.
